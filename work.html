03 — Mage of Blades

Art directing for the Zokio NFT universe.

Character Art Direction · Illustration Review · Zokio NFT Collection

The Brief

I art directed Mage of Blades for a NFT project. The piece, through several review passes with the illustrator, focused on pose construction, line of action, and final compositing rather than redrawing the character myself.

The goal throughout was to push every draft toward a stronger silhouette and a more legible sense of motion before any color or effects work began.

Red Lining: Drafts & Line of Action

Most of the Direction focused on facial structure; eyes that carry emotion, roots in the hairline, clearly defined features, and on posing: every draft was pushed toward a single readable line of action (an S, Z, or C curve pointing down through the figure) so the pose stays legible in silhouette and leads the eye through the panel.

Compositing — Enhancing Dynamics

The final review pass sat with compositors rather than illustrators. The same fight choreography was pushed through multiple lighting and effects treatments and judged side by side against a consistent set of metrics but essentially, how the panel feels to look at, not just whether it's technically correct.

"A pose only reads as heroic once the line of action, the silhouette, and the light are all pulling in the same direction."

04 — System Royale

Building a full cast at scale with an artist-led AI pipeline.

Character Design · Ensemble Cast · StoryTech Artist Initiative

Use of AI — an Ethical Creation Tool

Rather than adopt a public AI model trained on scraped artwork without consent, VoyceMe built its own Creation Tool trained only on material its own artists opted to contribute. Artists created the training material and reviewed every output, so the tool never replaced their judgment. It removed the hours of repetitive setup work standing between a concept and a finished panel.

The training material came from the artists themselves. On this project that meant the character turnarounds, pose sets, and finished art our team had already produced were what taught the tool the house style. Artists supplied the reference the model learned from, so anything it generated came back in a visual language they had authored, which is also why an artist could correct it quickly instead of fighting it.

In practice, that meant the tool handled background generation and an asset library of reusable environment pieces, freeing artists to spend their time on characters. Every image still passed through an artist for a final human touch before it shipped.

Artists Create the Training Material

The tool was trained on VoyceMe's own artists' work, with their consent, not scraped from the open internet.

Background Creation, so Artists Focus on Characters

An asset library of backgrounds and environments let artists build dynamic scenes quickly and spend their time on the cast instead.

Artists Maintain Quality

Every output was adjusted and approved by an artist before it shipped, keeping a human touch on every panel.

The Brief

This webtoon project I directed (System Royale) needed a large, visually distinct cast introduced quickly, without letting quality slip as the roster grew. So each character needed clean turnarounds, a readable silhouette, and enough personality to stand apart in group scenes. With tight deadlines around the corner we turned to AI to help with some of the heavy lifting.

To hit that volume on schedule, character and background work moved through VoyceMe's internal Creation Tool: an AI pipeline trained and supervised by artists rather than one that generates finished art on its own.

Solving it at that scale is what produced a new role on the team. Splitting a page into drawn characters, generated environments, and effects passes meant someone had to own how those layers came back together so I defined the StoryTech Artist, a compositing-first position modeled on animation and VFX pipelines rather than on traditional single-artist illustration.

05 — FableVerse

Giving generative storytelling a visual language.

CREATIVE DIRECTION · AI STORYTELLING · COMPOSITING · VISUAL SYSTEMS

The Story

FableVerse sits at the intersection of storytelling, fandom, identity, and generative AI. My role was to help define how that experience should feel creatively, not just how the images should look.

I worked across visual branding, prompt systems, product concepts, story-driven generation, and quality standards to help create an experience where users could enter a world, create characters, and generate imagery that felt more intentional and cinematic rather than random.

The Central Challenge

How do you turn an AI image generator into a storytelling experience?

Generation is more engaging when users are given context, narrative, and inspiration before they are asked to create.

Years of production compositing gave me a working method for creating scenes from piecing foreground and proportion correction, background reconstruction, matching light direction and color temperature between subject and environment, building depth in layers, and grading the whole frame to sit together. That craft is what closed the gap between what the model produced and what a cover needed to be.

Story-Driven Generation

Outputs produced under the prompt framework: cinematic framing, clear subject hierarchy, and environmental interaction instead of broad style descriptors.

Generation was the starting point, not the finish. Every cover here was composited by hand: I built them in layers, correcting anatomy and proportion, repairing hands and faces, rebuilding backgrounds and set dressing, unifying light direction and color temperature between the subject and the environment, adding depth through atmosphere and grain, then finishing with color grading and typography. The prompt framework got me a usable plate. Compositing is what made it a cover.

Character Adherence

Holding the same face across every scene.

One of the hardest problems was character adherence. AI often strays from the reference image. The face drifts, features shift, jewelry and hair details get reinvented scene to scene, and the illusion of a persistent character breaks. I was part of the team that worked on solving that: defining what identity actually consists of, building the reference and feedback loop that holds it, and reviewing generations against the source until the character stayed itself.

Reference

Generated scenes — same character, new context

Outfit, lighting, environment, and emotion all change. The braid, the earrings, the eye shape, and the face do not.

Reference

Generated scenes — same character, new poses and expressions

Shot size, camera angle, and expression range widely across the set. The glasses, hair shape, and plaid shirt stay locked to the reference.

Quality Guidance I Wrote

Base images. Single character, clearly visible, good lighting, sharp details, minimal background clutter.

Image context. Feed the previous output back in as the next input so consistency holds across a sequence.

From Conversation to Camera

The mapping that lets the engine read intent as direction: each conversational phrase resolves to a shot choice, and each shot choice resolves to a specific visual result.

Writing Context That Works

Less effective

"Make it better"

"Change stuff"

"Different"

"Update"

Effective

"Change the background to a sunset beach"

"Make her look worried and add rain"

"Move to a cozy coffee shop interior"

"Add dramatic lighting from the left"

Be specific about what should change, include emotional tone when relevant, describe lighting and atmosphere, reference previous context when building a narrative, and write in natural language. The same cinematic vocabulary that guided artists became the vocabulary that guides the model.

Artist-Led AI · A role I defined

The StoryTech Artist.

The left side is the drawn work: a hand-illustrated character on a clean background. The right side is the finished composite; that same drawing integrated with an AI-generated background and effects built in Clip Studio Paint. Magic circles, debris, glow, and atmospheric passes were layered around the figure, then light direction and color temperature were matched between character and environment so the two read as one image.

The role borrows directly from animation and VFX, where a compositor is the person who takes separately produced elements (characters, effects, backgrounds.) and merges them into a single believable frame.

Applied to webcomics, that becomes the StoryTech Artist: an artist who works across foreground, character, and background layers, deciding what gets drawn, what gets generated, what gets pulled from the asset library, and how all of it is unified into a finished piece. Compositing is the skill that makes the pipeline coherent instead of a pile of mismatched parts.
